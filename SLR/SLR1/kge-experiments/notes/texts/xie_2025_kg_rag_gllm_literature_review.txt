Abstract
The rapid growth of academic publications has intensified the challenges of conducting rigorous
literature reviews, pushing traditional and computational approaches to their limits. While Generative
Large Language Models (GLLMs) offer advanced capabilities for summarisation, abstraction, and
“synthesis”, their use in scholarly contexts is constrained by hallucination, limited context windows, and
weak evidential grounding. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) mitigates some of these issues by
anchoring outputs in verifiable sources, yet current applications remain focused on fact retrieval rather
than higher-level conceptual integration. To address this gap, we propose a knowledge graph-based
RAG–GLLM approach that combines structured knowledge representation with generative reasoning.
Knowledge graphs provide persistent context, reduce hallucination, and enable analysis across five
levels of literature review, from concepts to themes. Our research-in-progress outlines the conceptual
design, development plan, and early prototyping results, contributing a methodological foundation for
scalable, reliable, and theory-informed literature reviews in Information Systems research.

1 Toward Next-Generation Literature Reviews
Literature review is a foundational activity in the scientific process. It consolidates existing knowledge,
situates new research within established streams, and identifies emerging opportunities for theoretical
and practical advancement (Webster and Watson 2002). However, the exponential growth in academic
publications, driven by improved research infrastructure, global collaboration, and increasingly
powerful research tools, has fundamentally altered both the scale and complexity of this task. Traditional
literature review approaches, whether narrative (Baumeister and Leary 1997), systematic (e.g., Recker
et al. 2019), or bibliometric (Donthu et al. 2021), were developed in an era when publication volumes
were more manageable and analytical tools were primarily manual. These methods now face increasing
strain, often forcing researchers to choose between breadth of coverage and depth of synthesis. The
challenge is no longer simply to summarize existing work, but also to produce theoretically informed
integrations that connect disparate findings, identify gaps, and generate new avenues for inquiry.
Over the past two decades, computational text analysis methods such as Latent Semantic Analysis (LSA)
and Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) have been used to partially automate literature mapping and topic
identification (Indulska et al. 2012; Lee et al. 2010). These approaches detect statistical co-occurrence
patterns and organise terms into latent topics, offering scalable ways to summarise large corpora.
However, they are fundamentally restricted to surface-level statistical associations. Because they lack a
deep semantic understanding of context, their outputs often require extensive human interpretation to
connect identified patterns to theoretical constructs or to integrate across heterogeneous streams of
literature (e.g., Sidorova et al. 2008). This “last mile” problem, transforming statistical patterns into
theoretically informed synthesis, remains largely manual. These methods may help address the
challenge of scale, but are limited in conceptual depth in analysing literature.
Recent advances in natural language processing have introduced Generative Large Language Models
(GLLMs) such as ChatGPT, which go beyond statistical topic modelling by leveraging deep contextual
embeddings and vast pre-trained knowledge (de Kok 2025). In addition to processing and generating
natural language with remarkable fluency, GLLMs can integrate semantic understanding with advanced
analytical tasks such as summarisation, concept extraction, and relationship mapping. Crucially, they
can detect latent semantic patterns that extend beyond surface-level keyword or co-occurrence analysis,
enabling more conceptually rich and theoretically relevant interpretations. In this capacity, GLLMs offer
a more powerful and flexible alternative to traditional computational methods; one that can move
literature reviews beyond descriptive topic aggregation toward richer, integrated, and theoretically
informed synthesis1 (e.g., Tuczek et al. 2025).
Despite their potential, the use of GLLMs for literature review is constrained by several major
limitations. Chief among these is hallucination, the tendency of models to generate plausible but
factually incorrect statements (Wasserkrug et al. 2024). This is especially problematic in scholarly
contexts, where traceable evidence and logical rigour are essential. Additional limitations include
limited context windows for analysis (An et al. 2024), which restricts GLLMs’ ability to process extensive
bodies of literature as a whole, and the absence of robust evaluation metrics for assessing both the factual
accuracy and the theoretical coherence of generated analyses. Consequently, although GLLMs can
produce fluent and seemingly insightful outputs, their reliability as stand-alone tools for rigorous
academic synthesis remains debatable.
A promising response to these challenges is Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), which grounds
generative outputs in verifiable external source material (Arslan et al. 2024; Wasserkrug et al. 2024). By
coupling GLLMs with retrieval mechanisms anchored in trusted and verifiable academic databases, RAG
mitigates hallucination and enhances transparency. For example, the RAG-powered system PaperQA
retrieves and evaluates relevant paragraphs from full-text scientific articles and uses them as grounded
context to inform answer generation, ensuring that outputs remain traceable to the original literature
and improving factual reliability in scientific question-answering tasks (Lála et al. 2023). While RAG
improves factual reliability, it does not capture conceptual relationships, as it relies on retrieving similar
text embeddings, and the results are often fragmented and lack integration.
To overcome this limitation and promote the responsible use of GLLMs in literature review (and,
ultimately, in scientific knowledge discovery), we propose a knowledge graph-based RAG-GLLM

2 The Literature
2.1 Generative Large Language Models for Literature Review
Generative Large Language Models (GLLMs) represent an evolution of large language models (LLMs)
that combine deep contextual understanding with the ability to produce novel textual outputs (de Kok
2025). The generative nature of GLLMs allows researchers to explore new modes of knowledge discovery
and hypothesis generation. By integrating retrieved or structured information into coherent outputs,
GLLMs go beyond simple pattern recognition or statistical summarisation. In research contexts, this
positions GLLMs as a promising tool for tasks requiring both semantic comprehension and creative
synthesis, such as semantic analysis (e.g., Yoganarasimhan and Iakovetskaia 2024), scenario planning
(e.g., Chang et al. 2024), and hypothesis development (e.g., Zhou et al. 2024).
In the context of literature review, GLLMs have been applied across multiple stages. In the literature
search and retrieval process, GLLMs can understand research queries, expand search terms
semantically, and retrieve more comprehensive and relevant datasets (Bernard et al. 2025). During
screening and selection, GLLMs have been used to automate laborious filtering tasks, interpreting
human-defined inclusion or exclusion criteria and labelling articles as “accept” or “reject” (Dennstädt et
al. 2024). After filtering the literature, GLLMs can help generate summaries or structured data about
the literature dataset, for example, summarising work in themes and topics (e.g., ChatGPT Deep
Research) or extracting information based on human queries (Tang et al. 2024). Further, GLLMs have
been explored for knowledge discovery, such as defining concepts (Tuczek et al. 2025), mapping
conceptual relationships (Taleb et al. 2024), and generating research hypotheses (Tong et al. 2024).
Despite these promising applications, evidence highlights limitations in reliability and factual accuracy.
A key challenge is hallucination, where models generate plausible but incorrect information. For
example, Delgado-Chaves et al. (2025) reported that GLLMs misclassified up to 70% of papers
compared to human reviewers. Similarly, Elicit, a GLLM-based system for systematic literature review,
was found to underperform in extracting information from 50% of sampled studies relative to human
evaluation (Bianchi et al. 2025). These shortcomings often arise from the probabilistic nature of GLLM
generation and constraints such as limited context windows, leading to inaccurate information.
Given that literature review and knowledge discovery constitute the foundational core of scientific
research, requiring high levels of accuracy, coherence, and reliability, addressing the hallucination
problem is essential. Only by mitigating these limitations can the creative potential of GLLMs be
responsibly harnessed to support rigorous, theory-informed literature review.

2.2 Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is a framework that combines GLLMs with external knowledge
retrieval to overcome its intrinsic limitations. GLLMs can generate fluent text but often “hallucinate”
facts or rely on outdated training data. RAG addresses this by retrieving relevant external information
(e.g., from scientific papers or databases) and appending it to the GLLM’s context, thereby grounding

the generation in up-to-date, factual information. In practice, a user query is used to search an external
document corpus (retrieval), the top results are concatenated with the GLLM query (augmentation), and
then the GLLM generates an answer based on this extended prompt (generation) (Wasserkrug et al.
2024). This process reduces the model’s reliance on parametric memory and significantly enhances
factual accuracy and relevance of responses, especially in knowledge-intensive domains.
Beyond its technical refinements (Gao et al. 2023; Huang and Huang 2024), previous studies have
highlighted RAG’s versatility across domains such as medicine, finance, technology, and communication
(Arslan et al. 2024). Applications span tasks from question answering and information retrieval to text
analysis, report generation, and decision support. Within academic research, RAG has been explored in
literature-related tasks, including answering scholarly questions (Lála et al. 2023), summarising articles
(Agarwal et al. 2024), and grounding citations in generated text (Ye et al. 2023). These applications
illustrate RAG’s promise but also expose a gap: most reviewed existing systems remain tailored to factchecking or descriptive summarisation, rather than supporting higher-level conceptual synthesis that is
essential for theory-building in Information Systems and related fields. For instance, Lála et al. (2023)
demonstrate a tool that answers specific factual queries (e.g., identifying which molecules are associated
with certain macrophage responses), but such fact retrieval falls short of enabling integrated, theorydriven knowledge development.
A further limitation lies in the types of knowledge sources used. Current RAG implementations typically
rely on broad, publicly available corpora, such as Wikipedia, PubMed abstracts, or Amazon book
reviews, that are not optimized for scholarly synthesis (Arslan et al. 2024). While these sources provide
breadth, they lack the structured depth, domain specificity, and theoretical framing required for
academic literature reviews. Moreover, most approaches treat retrieval as a one-off process, appending
raw documents to prompts without structuring or iteratively refining them. Few studies have explored
the design of discipline-specific retrieval corpora, nor have they experimented with looped feedback
architectures where GLLM outputs are fed back into the retrieval pipeline for progressive knowledge
refinement. For literature review and knowledge discovery, where accuracy, conceptual coherence, and
theoretical integration are paramount, these omissions represent critical research challenges.

3.1 Literature Review Tasks and Their Challenges

encoded, and debated at multiple levels of abstraction (Watson et al. 2024). For a literature review to be

undifferentiated sources. We conceptualize the review process as spanning five analytical levels—
concept, relationship, model, theory, and theme—each with distinct goals and challenges (see a

Textual extraction is the most basic capability of GLLMs: it locates relevant spans such as definitions,
measures, associations, or theoretical framings within papers. Applied consistently across the five
review levels, extraction answers the fundamental question: “What does this paper say about x?”
However, similarity-based retrieval alone is insufficient; returned spans must be explicit and verifiable
to ensure scholarly rigour.
Semantic abstraction builds on extraction by normalising and compressing evidence so it can be
compared across studies. This enables reviewers to align varied definitions, distinguish causal from
correlational claims, or summarise competing models and theories. At higher levels, abstraction
supports concise narratives about field development. Yet, it carries risks: paraphrasing may smooth over
disagreements or introduce unsupported links. To remain reliable, abstractions must always be
annotated with references to their source text.
Knowledge synthesis integrates extracted and abstracted evidence across documents into higher-order
outputs such as reconstructed models, theory maps, and thematic narratives. While synthesis is
powerful, it is fragile—overly dependent on representative evidence and deliberate counterevidence
checks. Without these safeguards, integrative claims risk amplifying bias or overstating consensus.
Despite these capabilities, conventional GLLMs face recurring limitations: hallucination, loss of context
through chunking, lack of uncertainty signals, and weak provenance. These issues can yield persuasive
but untrustworthy syntheses that fall short of academic standards. Our design therefore emphasises (1)
token-level provenance, (2) adaptive chunking to preserve context, (3) evidence-constrained prompt
engineering, (4) iterative retrieval–generation cycles that build persistent knowledge graphs, and (5)
human-in-the-loop interventions to ensure reliability.

4 Research design
We propose a staged, multi-level process to develop and evaluate our RAG–GLLM approach. This
process builds directly on the three core capabilities of GLLMs and applies them across the five levels of
literature analysis. At each level, research activities will be organised into two stages: development
(architecture, prompts, and workflows) and evaluation (task benchmarks and expert assessment).
Following the high-level design of the RAG–GLLM pipeline (Figure 1), the developmental tasks include:
(1) constructing a knowledge graph tailored for literature review, with nodes representing extracted
entities (concepts, relationships, models, theories, themes) and edges capturing their relationships; (2)
designing the RAG process so that GLLMs can reason over the knowledge graph while maintaining
access to source-level evidence; (3) establishing mechanisms for verification and feedback loops, where
generated outputs are checked and inserted back to the knowledge graph (with human inventions); and
(4) creating a final synthesis process to generate literature review outputs.

In the evaluation stage, we will empirically assess the RAG–GLLM approach through comparative
experimentation. Specifically, we will benchmark it against (1) a baseline using simple, non-retrieval
GLLM prompting, and (2) traditional manual literature review methods. These evaluations will focus on
three dimensions of performance: efficiency (time and resource requirements) (Vindrola-Padros and
Johnson 2020), accuracy (fidelity of extracted and synthesised knowledge) (Levitt et al. 2021), and
creativity (ability to surface novel connections or research gaps) (Amabile et al. 1996). Both quantitative
measures (e.g., precision, recall, F1, inter-rater agreement) and qualitative assessments (e.g., expert
evaluation of review outputs) will be employed.
We are currently developing the first prototype of our RAG–GLLM literature review approach, with a
particular focus on concept extraction. This stage is foundational because accurate identification of key
constructs underpins the subsequent relationship, model, theory, and theme analyses. Our initial
development has concentrated on the first two core development tasks (see Figure 2). To evaluate its
feasibility, we conducted a pilot test using one research article (Rai et al. 2009). Results indicate that
our approach substantially outperforms a non-retrieval GLLM baseline (see Table 3). While the baseline
produced a large set of loosely related concepts with low precision and recall, the RAG–GLLM method
retrieved nearly all core constructs present in the study’s theoretical model with markedly higher
accuracy (Precision = 43%, Recall = 93%, F1 = 59%). Some noise remains, attributable to the use of an
intentionally simple prompt. We are now refining prompt engineering following de Kok (2025) and
Wasserkrug et al. (2024) to improve both precision and contextual relevance. These early findings
demonstrate that the integration of knowledge graphs with retrieval can significantly enhance the
reliability of concept extraction, thereby highlighting the utility of our proposed RAG-GLLM approach.

This research contributes methodological and practical value to the IS discipline. Methodologically, we
introduce a novel knowledge-based RAG-GLLM approach that enforces provenance, supports iterative
synthesis, and scales across multiple levels in literature reviews. Practically, our approach addresses a
pressing challenge faced by scholars in an era of exponential publication growth. By improving both
efficiency and reliability, our design has the potential to reduce barriers to theory development and
accelerate evidence-based knowledge discovery. In doing so, this research lays the groundwork for a new
generation of AI-augmented scholarly practices that balance scalability with scientific rigour.
