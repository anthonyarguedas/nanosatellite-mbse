# Reliability, Availability, Maintainability, and Safety: methods and tools for space missions

</div>

S. Radu $ ^{1} $ F. Cosson $ ^{1} $

Published online: 8 March 2024

$ \textcircled{c} $ The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Council of European Aerospace Societies 2024

Reliability, Availability, Maintainability, and Safety (RAMS) discipline is essential to ensure a highly reliable, robust, and safe service of all types of missions, may they be in naval, automotive, aeronautical or space area. As a result, this field is carefully covered in all engineering domains with the critical purpose of ensuring achievement of mission objectives, proper quantification of risk and most importantly, safe design, development, and operation. All global players are using the RAMS tools and methods to better improve the design of their engineering systems and ensure that the likelihood of failure is kept at a minimum acceptable.

Over the years, the whole space field has experienced an exceptional progress mainly due to the advancement in industrial technologies, development of space-quality product lines but perhaps, more importantly, miniaturization and overall digitalization. The consistent advancement of technology for spacecraft components/units instinctively anticipates more complex and ambitious goals or missions. However, one main driving factor continues to be cost.

RAMS field of engineering even when focused on space is wide and could be comparable to System Engineering. The RAMS discipline is transversal across the field of engineering, such as:

a) It tackles hardware-level engineering like equipment and overall electrical architecture of all types of units b) Subsystems such as a multitude of units put together with the scope of providing one or multiple functions (e.g., power the spacecraft)

c) Dedicated payloads with the scope of achieving an objective (e.g., gather meteorological data)

d) System which is the addition of all subsystems and payloads put together

e) Fault Detection Isolation and Recovery (FDIR) system design and process, which is heavily based on dependability (RAM)

f) Mission, mainly when we discuss about probability of success and overall Space Debris Mitigations Requirements (SDMR) related to RAMS (e.g., accidental breakup, reliability of disposal, etc.)

g) Product and quality assurance. The subject can cover all directions of space engineering and as such, considerable work and need for alignment is constantly being identified.

Gaps or alignment between RAMS and the following fields are currently under careful research worldwide, but more specifically under the European Space Agency (ESA) supervision:

- RAMS for equipment to system engineering-level alignment and proper selection of design—such as ensuring proper use of methods and tools to adequately perform trade-offs and choose the most suitable system in all missions. This is being performed by identifying from very low levels what is the most suitable choice of components, in terms of quality, number (i.e., redundancy), mass/volume, cost, that would lead to a design with an acceptable risk.

- RAMS and FDIR alignment by ensuring that RAMS is properly used for the design and development of FDIR process across all missions.

- RAMS and Model-Based System Engineering (MBSE) though ensuring that all RAMS viewpoints are being concurrently developed and evolving with the MBSE world and overall ensuring that a proper Model-Based Mission Assurance (MBMA) methodology is ready for use.

- Development of RAMS map which aims at ensuring proper alignment between all fields of engineering and highlighting the benefits and proper use of RAMS methodologies and tools tailored per type of mission.

- Return of Experience (REX) by gathering data from ongoing missions to decide if life extension is possible or disposal is to be performed based on the newly computed reliability using the on-board health monitoring and failures.

- REX for missions that have ended life, to better understand if the RAMS computations during design and development have predicted well enough the overall RAMS results post-mission.

- SDMR and compliance by ensuring that RAMS tools and methods are properly used to show compliance to the SDMR, this being particularly critical in new types of missions such as Active Debris Removal (ADR) or In Orbit Servicing (IOS). Ensuring that the RAMS field contributes to the zero debris policy.

- Development and use of RAMS methods to assess Acceptable Collision Probability Level (ACPL). This is being researched specifically for missions with higher risk of collision (e.g., ADR, IOS) by making use of tools such as Fault Tree Analysis (FTA) that may contribute to the assessment and quantification of combinations of failures that may lead to collision.

- Use of Artificial Intelligence (AI) to perform failure prognostics and perform recovery actions in such a way to increase availability of core subsystems or payloads.

- Use of AI for other aspects that may help RAMS such as exploitation of data mining techniques and anomaly reports filling to better support operations of satellites.

- Lastly but not least, constant identification of more suitable methods that could provide a more accurate estimation of reliability of components.

This is the first Special Issue on Reliability Availability Maintainability and Safety for space missions within the CEAS Space Journal and it provides the unique opportunity to explore, understand, fill in the gaps and suggest improvements within the RAMS field for space missions. Most of the collected papers have been based on contributions provided to the first ESA RAMS conference held in June 2022 which represented the largest RAMS multidisciplinary meeting of experts across all Europe. The conference will be held every 2 years and it represents a pre-eminent event for sharing about the advances in the RAMS European field. The topics that were and usually are very much present at the conference and covered by this special edition are related to: RAMS new methodologies, RAMS relation to FDIR systems, illustration and tailoring of RAMS analyses at various levels per type of missions, RAMS development within MBSE and MBMA, RAMS/FIDR practices for deep space

missions using small factor satellites or usage of RAMS tools with AI. These are some of the interesting constituents of the CEAS RAMS special edition.

In the first paper, the reader will have a presentation of the "RAMS Map" which constitutes an extremely useful tool for space system engineers and mission analysts. It provides a generic view based on standards, expert judgment, and overall experience acquired from a variety of past and ongoing space missions with focus on spacecraft. This "RAMS Map" can be tailored per each mission and several examples are provided as part of the paper to better show the adaptability and versatility of the "RAMS map". The developed and presented work represents an effective way to show, explain, and understand the way the RAMS methods should be used within a space mission to be able to provide valuable input for design and development to System Engineering and FDIR Engineering.

In the second paper, the RAMS is being introduced within the MBSE world through COMPASTA which is a research activity performed to integrate all the functionalities of COMPASS and TASTE toolsets. COMPASS is a tool for MBSE developed in a series of ESA studies from 2008 to 2016. It integrates various formal techniques for automated verification, based on model checking. COMPASS supports and automates activities related to requirements engineering, contract-based design, functional verification, safety assessment, FDIR specification and effectiveness analysis. TASTE is a development environment for real-time embedded systems, developed under the initiative of ESA, and following the MBSE approach. The novelty of COMPASTA consists in providing a full end-to-end coherent tool chain covering system design, verification, RAMS analyses, HW/SW implementation, deployment, and testing.

The third paper aims at presenting the use of MBSE engineering for the socio-technical interactive systems. Command and control rooms are socio-technical systems that gather many devices and software of different types and aim at supporting groups of operators to manage missions and systems in a collaborative way. They include interactive systems, who particularly support operators in controlling the local or remote systems. The paper presents the results of the application of the approach to the model-based design and development of collaborative application for the management of collision risks between satellites and space objects.

The fourth paper of the special issue looks at RAMS utilization for NewSpace missions such as CubeSats that have complex scientific objectives to achieve in harsh environments. The specificities of the RAMS analysis and FDIR concept for this class of small satellite missions are highlighted in the paper, looking in particular at aspects such as the improvement of reliability while maintaining the CubeSat philosophy, the tuning of mission and system requirements in view of facilitating the design and implementation

of the FDIR concept, and the current gaps within the RAMS/ FDIR body of knowledge. The view of how to perform RAMS analyses for such missions is deemed complex for CubeSats; however, the paper shows that this can be performed in a beneficial manner.

The fifth paper is looking at the use of RAMS in the world of AI which is currently emerging in the space field as well. As the volume of telemetry data generated by satellites and other complex systems continues to grow, there is a pressing need for more efficient and accurate anomaly detection methods. Current techniques often rely on human analysis and preset criteria, presenting several challenges including the necessity for expert interpretation and continual updates to match the dynamic mission environment. This paper critically examines the use of Deep Anomaly Detection (DAD) methods in addressing these challenges, evaluating their efficacy on real-world spacecraft telemetry data. It exposes multiple flaws in current DAD research, highlighting the tendency for performance results to be overestimated and suggesting that simpler methods can sometimes outperform more complex DAD algorithms.

The sixth paper looks at new methods of pushing reliability prediction practices in space applications, methods that are soon to be integrated within the European Cooperation for Space Standardization (ECSS) dossier, through a Reliability handbook. The paper provides a sneak-peak of what the handbook will contain through explaining the way to use the handbook on a concrete challenging example: a mega constellation of CubeSats.

The seventh paper looks at the potential use of AI within the space field. As the volume of telemetry data generated by satellites, which are very complex systems, continues to grow with information such as failure rates, there is a pressing need for more efficient and accurate anomaly detection methods. The existing techniques often rely on analysis and preset criteria, presenting many challenges such as the necessity of expert interpretation and updates to match the,

often difficult and complex, mission environment. This paper examines the use of DAD methods with respect to addressing challenges in implementing AI-based methods within the space field.

The last paper of this special issue looks at how a mission should end and the topic is related to how RAMS analyses should be used in support to end-of-life decision for satellite disposal. This paper summarizes some of the studies performed by Thales Alenia Space with the aim of deriving how RAMS analyses and criteria could support a better risk-awareness decision on satellite end of life and thus to higher compliance to SDMR; and how this could contribute to the success of space missions and to a more sustainable use of space.

Concluding the presentation of this Special Issue, we would like to acknowledge the pivotal work of the editors, guest co-editors, reviewers, and authors. The increased importance that RAMS has acquired within the space community over the past years is exceptional and has led to the proposal of this special edition on RAMS within CEAS Space Journal. Consequently, we truly hope that this first ever Special Issue on RAMS of the CEAS Space Journal will make an interesting and inspiring reading to all RAMS professionals and enthusiasts.
